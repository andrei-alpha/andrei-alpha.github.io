Project 02 • Geometric Optical Illusion

3D Cubes Optical Illusion Board

A 45 cm × 24 cm × 2.5 cm board creating a striking three-dimensional tumbling blocks illusion. Engineered through a precise 60° tri-tonal tessellation of Swiss Walnut, European Beech, and Alpine Maple / Spruce in Zürich.

Project Specifications & Parameters

Final Dimensions 45.0 cm length × 24.0 cm width × 2.5 cm thickness
Visual Effect 3D Isometric Tumbling Cubes (Reversible Necker Cube optical illusion)
Geometry Pattern 60° / 120° Rhomboid Tessellation (Rhombille tiling) arranged in hexagonal trios
Timber & Tonal Roles
  • • Alpine Maple / Spruce (Acer / Picea) — Top Face (Light highlight, catching ambient overhead light)
  • • European Beech (Fagus sylvatica) — Right Face (Medium half-tone, warm golden body)
  • • Swiss Walnut (Juglans regia) — Left Face (Deep shadow plane, creating isometric depth)
Adhesive Titebond III Ultimate (waterproof, food-contact compliant aliphatic resin)
Primary Machinery & Jigs Festool TKS 80 EBS table saw with miter gauge & crosscut sled, Jet drum sander, Bessey parallel & pipe clamps, hardwood cauls, Glue-Bot applicator
Sanding Regimen Sequential random orbital sanding: 80 → 120 → 180 → 240 (water popped) grit with hard backing pads
Finishing System Deep USP food-grade mineral oil saturation bath followed by organic beeswax conditioning butter
Board Function Heavy-duty charcuterie, bread cutting, and dramatic culinary presentation board / desk centerpiece
Workshop Origin Zürich, Switzerland

Woodworking Geometry

The Anatomy of the 3D Cube Illusion

Why this pattern tricks human spatial perception and the strict geometric tolerances required in the workshop.

Phase 01 Optical Physics & Tonal Value

The Three-Value Lighting Principle & Rhombille Tiling

The 3D illusion relies fundamentally on value contrast rather than color contrast alone. For the human visual cortex to perceive three-dimensional volume on a flat surface, the brain demands three distinct light values aligned with an assumed directional light source:

  • Top Plane (Maple / Spruce): Reflects maximum incident light (~80% reflectance). Provides a clean, bright highlight canvas that catches overhead illumination.
  • Mid-Tone Plane (Beech): Represents diffuse ambient light (~45% reflectance). The dense, warm honey-amber of European Beech acts as the tonal bridge between highlight and deep shadow.
  • Shadow Plane (Walnut): Simulates the occluded face (~15% reflectance). Rich Swiss Walnut provides deep espresso and chocolate hues, grounding the shadow side of each cube.

When assembled into 60°/120° diamonds meeting at a single vertex, the brain automatically reconstructs an isometric cube floating in three dimensions. Depending on cognitive focus, the cubes can appear to tumble toward you or recede away—the classic reversible Necker Cube phenomenon.

Phase 02 Trigonometric Tolerances

The Compounding Angle Challenge: Precision at 60°

Unlike 90-degree square cutting boards (such as the Beech & Purpleheart grid), the 3D cubes board requires cuts at exactly 60 degrees. A deviation of just 0.1° per cut compounds sixfold at every hexagonal junction, creating a 0.6° gap error. Multiplied across 8 rows, an inaccurate miter gauge will leave gaping seams that no amount of clamp pressure can force shut without tearing the wood fibers.

Workshop Strategy: The Two-Stage Laminated Strip Technique

Rather than cutting and attempting to glue hundreds of individual loose diamond rhomboids, woodworkers laminate long strips of Walnut, Beech, and Maple edge-glued in parallel, then slice the entire laminated slab across the grain at exactly 60 degrees. Shifting adjacent sliced strips by exactly one segment automatically aligns the tumbling cubes, ensuring laser-straight glue lines and saving dozens of hours of perilous assembly.

Step 01 Initial Lamination

Three-Species Strip Lamination & Blank Assembly

The foundation of the optical illusion begins by milling long, perfectly uniform strips of Swiss Walnut, European Beech, and pale Alpine Maple/Spruce. Each strip was planed to identical thickness and jointed square on the edge. The strips were then arranged in a repeating tonal sequence—Walnut (shadow), Beech (half-tone), and Maple (highlight)—and edge-glued face-to-face to create the initial multi-layer striped blank.

Clamping was executed using heavy-duty Bessey pipe and parallel jaw clamps spaced closely across the workbench. Uniform pressure along the entire length was critical to avoid cupping or starving the joints, ensuring that every subsequent slice would share identical strip widths.

Alternating Walnut, Beech, and Maple strips under even clamping pressure to form the master laminated blank. Click to enlarge
Woodworker's Insight: Strip Width Calibration

In an isometric projection, the vertical height of a 60° rhombus is related to the cut thickness by the sine of 60° (\(\sqrt{3}/2 pprox 0.866\)). If the strip width and the subsequent crosscut slicing thickness do not match the exact geometric ratio, the rhomboids become stretched or squashed, transforming the crisp 3D isometric cubes into warped hexagonal prisms.

Step 02 Precision Angle Slicing

60-Degree Angle Crosscutting on the Table Saw

Once the laminated blank was cured and planed clean on both faces, it was brought to the table saw to be crosscut into angled segments. The miter gauge was dialed in to precisely 60 degrees relative to the blade. A sacrificial fence and stop block ensured that every sliced strip emerged with identical segment width and pristine, tearout-free edges.

Each pass through the carbide blade sliced across the striped blank, yielding long parallelogram strips where the Walnut, Beech, and Maple sections were skewed into 60°/120° rhomboids. Clean, chatter-free cuts here are vital: even minor blade wobble will telegraph into hollows during the second glue-up.

Angled cuts transform linear stripes into parallelogram segments with 60° diamond facets. Click to enlarge
Step 03 Dry Assembly & Alignment

Dry-Fitting the Tumbling Blocks Pattern

This is the defining moment in the build: laying out the sliced parallelogram strips side-by-side on the assembly bench. By staggering each successive strip along the 60-degree axis by exactly one rhomboid segment, the individual diamonds align to form three-faceted hexagons.

Instantly, the two-dimensional diagonal strips vanish, replaced by the striking optical illusion of stacked, three-dimensional cubes tumbling through space. During dry-fit, the strips were inspected under raking light to verify that wood grain flowed harmoniously and that no strip had accidentally been inverted.

Offsetting adjacent strips by one unit causes the three species to meet at 120° vertices. Click to enlarge
Step 04 Tolerance Inspection

Vertex Registration & Close Seam Inspection

Before applying adhesive, a rigorous inspection of every intersection was performed. In a proper isometric tessellation, three distinct diamonds meet at each shared vertex: a bright top diamond, a warm mid-tone side diamond, and a dark shadow diamond.

Any drift in blade angle or thickness variation would show up immediately as stepped corners or gaps at these three-point hubs. Fine pencil reference lines were marked across the strips to ensure perfect longitudinal registration during the frantic open-time of the glue-up.

Critical verification: ensuring sharp three-facet vertices meet without axial creep or offset. Click to enlarge
Step 05 The First Glue-Up

Hand-Assembling Diamonds into Hexagon Cubes

With all angles cut and dry-fit tolerances inspected, the critical first assembly phase began: gluing the individual diamond rhomboids into hexagonal cube units. In an isometric tumbling blocks pattern, each 3D cube is formed from three 60°/120° diamonds—one Alpine Spruce (top highlight), one European Beech (warm mid-tone), and one Swiss Walnut (deep shadow)—meeting precisely at a central 120-degree vertex.

Clamping three loose diamond facets with conventional screw or parallel bar clamps is practically impossible without slipping. The acute 60° angles act as wedges: tightening mechanical clamp pressure forces the pieces to scissor and slide violently out of alignment.

To solve this, the first glue-up was completed entirely by hand. Titebond III aliphatic resin was applied to the meeting 60-degree edges of the three diamonds. Each hexagonal trio was then gripped firmly between the fingers, applying steady manual pressure for 1 to 2 minutes per block until the adhesive developed enough green tack strength to hold its shape without shifting or sliding off-plane.

This manual bonding was repeated block by block, unit by unit, for every single hexagon required across the board. In total, it was an extraordinarily time-consuming, finger-tiring ordeal that demanded immense focus and patience—yet it allowed tactile micro-adjustments to ensure the top faces remained perfectly coplanar and the three-facet vertices stayed crisp.

All 3-diamond hexagons assembled by hand, held under manual pressure for 1–2 minutes per block until tack. Click to enlarge
Maker's Retrospective: Better & More Efficient Assembly Techniques

While holding each block by hand for 1–2 minutes ensured perfect joint registration without clamp slippage, it is undeniably slow and labor-intensive. For future iterations, several superior production techniques exist:

  • Elastic Tape Banding: Laying the three diamonds flat in a row on strips of high-tack blue painter's tape, applying glue, folding them into the 3D hexagon, and stretching the tape tightly across the closing seam. The elastic tension of the tape pulls all three joints inward simultaneously with uniform radial pressure—eliminating manual holding entirely.
  • 60° V-Channel Clamping Sled: Constructing a dedicated wooden V-trough angled at 60 degrees and lined with polypropylene packing tape. A small wooden wedge clamped from behind presses the three diamonds together against the fence.
  • Pre-Laminated Angled Strip Slicing: Gluing the three wood species into continuous long 60-degree laminated blanks before crosscutting, creating pre-bonded rows of diamonds in a single cut.
Step 06 Second Glue-Up

Master Lamination with Clamping Cauls

With all individual 3-diamond hexagons cured from the first glue-up, the project advanced to the second glue-up: joining the entire field of hexagonal cubes into a unified, monolithic master panel. When lateral clamping force is applied against 60-degree angled joints across multiple rows, the hydraulic wedge effect causes the panel to want to slide along the joints and buckle upward like a tent.

To counter this, heavy hardwood clamping cauls wrapped in packing tape (to prevent glue bonding) were positioned across the top and bottom faces of the assembly. Vertical F-clamps locked the cauls down flat to the bench, while Bessey parallel jaw clamps applied controlled lateral pressure, locking all strips into a single, dead-flat monolithic panel without slippage.

Rigid top and bottom cauls keep the 60° angled strips flat on the bench while lateral clamps close the seams. Click to enlarge
Step 07 Curing & Inspection

Release from Clamps & Glue Squeeze-Out Analysis

After a full 24-hour cure under clamp tension, the cauls and clamps were released. The raw glued slab emerged with its characteristic zigzag, stepped perimeter—the natural result of offsetting the 60° parallelogram strips.

Examining the cured glue beads along each seam confirmed consistent squeeze-out across the entire panel, proving complete adhesive coverage with zero starved voids. The cured glue beads were scraped off with a sharp carbide cabinet scraper before machine surfacing to protect cutter knives.

Zigzag perimeter edges before trimming, with hardened Titebond III squeeze-out ready for scraping. Click to enlarge
Step 08 Precision Squaring

Perimeter Trimming on the Festool Table Saw

To turn the raw stepped panel into a clean, rectilinear cutting board, the board was moved to the workshop's precision saw station: the Festool TKS 80 EBS table saw (equipped with SawStop AIM safety technology).

Using a precision sliding crosscut fence, the jagged stepped ends were trimmed clean, establishing the first true 90-degree reference edge. The board was then rotated and rip-cut to establish parallel sides, finalizing the external dimensions at 45.0 cm × 24.0 cm. Visible in the shop background is the yellow Jet drum sander, ready for thickness calibration.

Festool TKS 80 table saw squaring the stepped edges; Jet drum sander visible in the background workshop. Click to enlarge
Step 09 Surfacing & Edge Prep

Drum Sanding, Edge Cauls & Surface Preparation

With the perimeter squared, the board underwent thicknessing and flattening on the Jet drum sander. Multiple light passes removed any residual surface height variations between adjacent strips, bringing the board down to a uniform 2.5 cm thickness while preserving sharp facet intersections.

Back on the workbench, edge cauls and Bessey parallel clamps were used for final stabilization and fine edge dressing. The board was then sanded progressively through 80 → 120 → 180 → 240 grit using random orbital sanders. Between 180 and 240 grit, the entire surface was "water popped" (wiped with a damp microfiber cloth) to raise crushed grain fibers, then sanded smooth with 240 grit for an ultra-velvety touch. A delicate 45° chamfer was routed along all perimeter edges and corners for ergonomic comfort.

Workbench staging: edge caul clamping setup alongside the Glue-Bot dispenser before final abrasive finishing. Click to enlarge
Step 10 The Finish Reveal

The Oil Pop — The First Centerline Brush Stroke

After hours of geometric planning, cutting, gluing, and meticulous sanding, the moment every woodworker waits for arrived: the application of the first finish coat. Using a clean natural bristle brush, pure USP food-grade mineral oil was painted straight down the center column of the dry, raw board.

The transformation is breathtaking. The muted, dusty-grey walnut instantly erupts into rich dark espresso; the pale beech blooms into warm honey-amber; and the spruce facets take on a clean, luminous gleam. The sharp boundary between the raw sanded flanks and the freshly oiled center creates an unforgettable visual spectacle on the workbench.

Instant contrast: the dry raw wood on the flanks versus the vibrant, oil-saturated cubes down the center line. Click to enlarge
Step 11 Deep Nourishment

Full Surface Saturation & 3D Illusion Depth

The mineral oil was applied generously over the entire top, bottom, and edge surfaces, allowing the end grain and long grain to drink in the oil until fully saturated. After wiping off excess oil and resting overnight, the board received a hot coat of handcrafted beeswax and botanical oil wood butter, buffed to a silky semi-gloss luster.

With the full surface saturated, the optical illusion reaches its full visual potency. The deep walnut shadow planes seem to recede inches below the surface, the beech facets provide warm solid body, and the top maple facets appear to catch a bright spotlight from above. Under any lighting condition, the flat board appears unmistakably as a three-dimensional field of stacked cubes.

The fully finished 45cm × 24cm board showing rich walnut shadows, golden beech, and bright top highlights. Click to enlarge
Step 12 The Heirloom in Service

The Finished Masterpiece in Its Natural Habitat

Here is the completed 3D Cubes Optical Illusion Board in its everyday environment—photographed on a modern dual-monitor desk in Zürich. Whether serving as a breathtaking culinary presentation board for artisanal sourdough and aged Swiss cheeses or standing as an executive desk pad and geometric conversation piece, the craftsmanship speaks for itself.

Every joint is tight, the surface is dead flat and velvety smooth to the touch, and the geometric precision of the 60° tessellation creates an enduring testament to patient, methodical woodworking.

Care & Maintenance Protocol

How to care for a multi-species geometric cutting board so it lasts generations.

What To Do

  • Hand wash both sides with mild dish soap and warm water immediately after use.
  • Towel dry thoroughly and stand on edge to air dry evenly on all sides.
  • Apply food-grade mineral oil once a month or whenever the timber looks pale or feels dry.
  • Reapply beeswax and botanical board butter every 3–4 months for rich water-repelling protection.

What Never To Do

  • Never put in the dishwasher: High heat, harsh detergents, and steam will dissolve the glue and warp the timber.
  • Never soak or leave submerged in the sink.
  • Never dry flat on a countertop (trapped moisture underneath causes cupping and joint stress).
  • Never use cooking oils like olive or canola (they turn rancid, sticky, and produce odors).
